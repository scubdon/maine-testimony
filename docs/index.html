Who testifies in Augusta?

Every bill in Maine gets a public hearing, and anyone can send the committee a letter. This project collected all of them posted since 2013, sorted out who wrote each one, read which side it took, and lined them up with what happened to the bill.

1 · Growth

More people are writing to the Legislature

Written testimony went from about letters in the Legislature (2013–14) to in the (2025–26). The number of hearing days barely moved, so each bill now hears from more people: the median bill drew letters then and now.

Most of the growth came from individuals writing in, the group shown in green. The 129th's jump came with a wave of form letters: a fifth of individuals' letters that session matched other letters on the same bill, the most of any Legislature.

2 · Who

Residents write most of the letters. Organizations write most of the rest.

The submission form has one free-text "organization" box. Most residents type their town into it; groups type their name. Sorting of those boxes gives individuals, organizations, government bodies and legislators.

Each group leans differently. Legislators testify mostly for bills (usually their own). Government agencies often take no side ("neither for nor against") and explain how a bill would work. Individuals oppose more often than any other group.

3 · Campaigns

The biggest turnouts are opposition campaigns

Most bills draw a handful of letters, split roughly 60/30 for and against. The few bills that draw hundreds look different: on the bills with 500+ letters, opposition outnumbers support about two to one.

Much of that volume is organized. The project groups near-identical letters on the same bill: letters fall into such groups. The largest, on the 132nd's e-cigarette bill, is copies of the same letter.

4 · Organizations

The regulars, and who they agree with

The Maine Municipal Association has testified more than any other group, on bills. Advocacy groups tend to pick a side; trade and government associations take "neither for nor against" far more often.

When two frequent groups both took a side on the same bill, how often was it the same side? Ordering the groups so that allies sit next to each other splits them into two blocs: business and industry associations on one end, labor, health and anti-poverty groups on the other. Environmental and municipal groups sit in between.

Individuals who name a Maine town can be placed on a map. Per resident, County writes the most and County the least: a -fold gap. The midcoast writes the most per resident; Portland and its suburbs send the most letters overall.

Most people write once. A few write constantly: of individuals sent a single letter, while the who sent 26 or more wrote of all individuals' letters. People are counted by first name, last name and town, so the same person writing from two towns counts twice.

6 · Outcomes

Does testimony track what happens to a bill?

Each bill's status page records its committee report and final outcome. Joining that to the testimony: of the closed bills with positioned testimony became law. Among the bills with at least five public voices, the pass rate falls steadily as the share opposed rises.

A "voice" here is one letter from anyone but a legislator, with each form-letter campaign counted once. Share opposed = oppose ÷ (support + oppose).

Once a committee decides, testimony stops mattering

Split the same bills by their committee report and the slope nearly vanishes. A bill with a unanimous "ought to pass" report becomes law about three times in four whether its testimony was 5% opposed or 95%. So heavy opposition goes with committees voting a bill down; after that, the floor follows the committee.

Party matters, and so does opposition

Sponsor party comes from Open States (128th Legislature on). Bills from the party that controlled the sponsor's chamber became law of the time; minority-party bills . Majority bills also drew less opposition ( opposed on average vs ). Within each side, opposition still goes with failure, and with the committee recommending against.

These are associations, not effects. Opponents mobilize where a bill might pass, and lawmakers and letter-writers react to the same things about a bill (its sponsor, its cost, who is asking). This data can't say whether testimony changed any vote.

7 · Method

How this was built, and how accurate it is

The Legislature's testimony page is a JavaScript shell over a few undocumented JSON endpoints. A polite scraper (one request per second, identifying user agent, no robots.txt-disallowed pages) pulled every hearing from the 126th Legislature on, then each letter's PDF. About 2.4% of PDFs are scans; those were OCR'd.

Who. The free-text organization box is sorted by ordered, auditable rules: a Maine town → individual; "Department of…", "Town of…" → government; known org names, acronyms and an alias table → organization. Which side. Ordered regular expressions read the letter's stated position; letters they can't place go to Claude Haiku, which must quote the sentence that states the stance (the quote is checked against the text). Outcomes come from each bill's status page; sponsor party from Open States.

Accuracy was measured on samples hand-labeled after tuning was finished:

submitter category correct (n = 300)
positions correct with the Claude fallback (n = 200; regex alone )
of positions right when one is given (rules alone)

Privacy. Most testimony comes from private people and often includes addresses and personal stories. This site and the public dataset contain no names of private individuals and none of the letters' text. Individuals appear only as a category and a town. Organizations and legislators appear by name.

Limits. The organization box is self-reported. Budget and unprinted-bill testimony uses another endpoint that isn't collected yet. Pass rates describe bills people testified on, not all bills. The Legislature notes its online record "may not represent a complete record of a hearing."

Data and code. Everything here is built from the project's GitHub repository: the scraper, the classifiers, and the public dataset in data/public/ (one row per submission, plus bill, organization and town summaries).
